01 — The Recovery Gap

Treatment has a protocol. Recovery does not end at discharge — it just stops having one.

Diagnosis, treatment and surgery follow well-mapped clinical pathways. What happens afterward — physically, emotionally, practically — is far less structured, and looks different for every patient. That gap, largely undesigned, is where Beyond Survival works.

Diagnosis

Clinical pathway begins. Information is dense, timelines move quickly.

Treatment

Chemotherapy, radiotherapy or hormone therapy, sequenced by the care team.

Surgery

Mastectomy or breast-conserving surgery, closely monitored and protocol-driven.

Hospital Discharge

Clinical oversight tapers. Formal support structures largely end here.

Undesigned

Recovery

Mobility, garments, body image, routine — mostly navigated alone.

Long-term Adaptation

Ongoing, individual, and rarely accounted for in the original care plan.

02 — Understanding Recovery

Seven things recovery actually involves

Physiotherapy session, natural light
01

Physical recovery

Shoulder mobility, scar tissue, and strength rebuilt gradually with guided exercise.

Range-of-motion exercise, close crop
02

Post-operative mobility

Arm and shoulder movement is often the first, most immediate recovery challenge.

Kitchen, everyday task, hands
03

Returning to daily routines

Cooking, dressing, lifting, working — ordinary tasks that require re-learning.

Mirror, soft focus, dignity-led
04

Body image

Adjusting to physical change is personal, ongoing, and deserves care in how it's discussed.

Fabric swatches, stitched seams
05

Choosing suitable garments

Post-surgical bras and prostheses need to fit well — practically and emotionally.

Clinic corridor, quiet architecture
06

Ongoing follow-up care

Check-ups continue well past discharge, often without a clear communicated schedule.

Support group, hands, notebook
07

Support networks

Peer groups, family and counselling shape recovery as much as clinical care does.

Why this matters

No two recoveries follow the same shape. Good design starts by admitting that.

03 — Designing Better Recovery

Beyond Survival is a design process, not a charity programme

We treat recovery as a design problem: rigorous, iterative, and grounded in direct contact with patients, surgeons and physiotherapists — resulting in products and programmes hospitals can actually adopt.

Input

Patient research

Interviews and shadowing with recovering patients across care stages.

Input

Hospital collaboration

Working alongside surgeons, oncology nurses and physiotherapists.

Method

Design thinking

Prototyping, testing and iterating rather than assuming solutions.

Output

Affordable products

Garments and aids designed for cost, comfort and accessibility.

Output

Community education

Guides and workshops that travel beyond the hospital walls.

04 — Recovery Collection

Four objects, each shaped by hundreds of conversations

Presented here as we develop them — not as a shop. Each is a working prototype, refined through hospital testing and patient feedback.

Post-Mastectomy

Object 01 — Garment
GRAINLINE↕SEAM A

Soft-seam, front-closing design for limited shoulder mobility in early recovery.

Purpose

Supportive, low-pressure garment worn immediately after surgery and through early recovery.

Who may benefit

Patients with limited arm mobility, drain tubes, or sensitive scar tissue.

Design considerations

Front closure, wide soft straps, no underwire, pocket for surgical drains.

Materials

Breathable cotton-modal blend, flat-locked seams, latex-free elastic.

Future development

Adjustable band sizing to extend fit range across a single garment.

External Breast Prosthesis

Object 02 — Aid
GRAINLINE↕SEAM B

Lightweight, low-cost alternative to silicone forms for everyday wear.

Purpose

Restores silhouette and weight balance for patients after mastectomy.

Who may benefit

Patients not pursuing reconstruction, or awaiting a later procedure.

Design considerations

Skin-safe materials, washable, weighted to sit naturally against the body.

Materials

Layered textile fill in a sealed, hypoallergenic outer shell.

Future development

Climate-adapted versions for warmer, humid regions.

Recovery Garment

Object 03 — Garment
GRAINLINE↕SEAM C

Everyday layer designed around scar sensitivity and range-of-motion limits.

Purpose

A comfortable outer layer for the weeks following discharge.

Who may benefit

Patients adjusting to reduced arm mobility during everyday tasks.

Design considerations

Side-seam access, minimal overhead dressing, soft interior facing.

Materials

Brushed cotton exterior, seam-free interior panel at the chest.

Future development

Piloting a version adapted for reconstructive surgery patients.

Educational Recovery Kit

Object 04 — Resource
GRAINLINE↕SEAM D

A printed guide and checklist, handed out at discharge alongside garments.

Purpose

Plain-language guidance for the first weeks and months at home.

Who may benefit

Patients and family members supporting day-to-day recovery.

Design considerations

Large type, illustrated exercises, space for personal notes.

Materials

Recycled uncoated paper stock, saddle-stitched binding.

Future development

Regional-language editions developed with partner hospitals.

05 — Research Process

Eight stages, worked in order, revisited often

Scroll sideways — the way you'd move through a pinboard of field notes and prototypes.

01

Patient conversations

Open interviews with recovering patients, recorded with consent and transcribed.

02

Hospital observations

Shadowing discharge routines and post-operative wards across partner sites.

03

Problem definition

Synthesising findings into specific, testable design problems.

04

Prototype sketches

Hand-drawn pattern pieces and construction notes, reviewed with clinicians.

05

Material testing

Fabric trials for breathability, seam strength and skin sensitivity.

06

User feedback

Wear-testing with patients across different recovery stages.

07

Iteration

Refining pattern, fit and materials against feedback, repeatedly.

08

Implementation

Handover to partner hospitals for distribution and ongoing evaluation.
